\section{Empirical Evaluation and Results}
\label{sec:results}

In this section, we present the empirical evaluation of the proposed lightweight, host-CPU prompt-complexity routing framework against the standard static serving baseline (\texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}, $Q4\_K\_M$). All measurements were captured under strict quiescent baseline controls and high-frequency NVML hardware power sampling on consumer-grade GPU hardware.

\subsection{Experimental Environment and Quiescent Power Profiling}
\label{subsec:hardware_setup}

All benchmark inferences were executed on an isolated \textbf{NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060} (Ada Lovelace microarchitecture, TSMC 4N process, 8~GB GDDR6 VRAM, 115~W TDP) driven by local Ollama serving engines. Dynamic GPU power $P(t)$ was sampled at high frequency ($f_s = 20\text{ Hz}$, $50\text{ ms}$ interval) using an asynchronous daemon thread interfacing with the NVIDIA Management Library (\texttt{pynvml}). 

To ensure rigorous isolation of dynamic inference energy from ambient platform power, a mandatory pre-flight quiescent baseline profiling period ($t_{\text{rest}} \ge 3.0\text{ s}$) was conducted prior to evaluation batches. The measured quiescent baseline idle power on the target GPU platform was:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{idle}} = 54.10 \pm 0.12\text{ W}
\end{equation}
Total workload electrical energy ($E_{\text{total}}$) and net dynamic inference energy ($E_{\text{net}}$) were calculated via trapezoidal integration over execution duration $[0, T]$:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \frac{P(t_k) + P(t_{k+1})}{2} \cdot (t_{k+1} - t_k)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{net}} = \max\left(0, E_{\text{total}} - (P_{\text{idle}} \times \Delta t_{\text{latency}})\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Workload Tier Distribution and Task Breakdown}
\label{subsec:task_breakdown}

The multi-class prompt router operates with calibrated confidence thresholds ($\tau_1 = 0.1, \tau_2 = 0.1$) guaranteeing a validation False Negative Rate $\text{FNR} < 5\%$. Under active serving, the router distributed incoming queries across three parameter tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (\texttt{Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct}, $Q8\_0$)}: Accounted for \textbf{16.7\%} of total volume, capturing low-complexity surface tasks (SST-2).
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (\texttt{Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct}, $Q4\_K\_M$)}: Accounted for \textbf{50.0\%} of total volume, absorbing intermediate summarization (CNN/DailyMail), natural language inference (MNLI), and extractive question answering (SQuAD v2.0).
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (\texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}, $Q4\_K\_M$)}: Accounted for \textbf{33.3\%} of total volume, reserved exclusively for multi-step algorithmic deduction (GSM8K) and Python code synthesis (HumanEval).
\end{itemize}

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Latency Profile and Router Execution Overhead}
\label{subsec:latency_results}

A common concern in dynamic serving architectures is whether online routing decision latency offsets downstream GPU execution gains. In our implementation, feature extraction (combining lexical heuristics and 384-dimensional dense sentence embeddings from \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) and classification were executed strictly on host CPU cores.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{figures/latency_profile.png}
\caption{Serving latency profile: End-to-end execution latency, Time-to-First-Token (TTFT), and host CPU routing overhead.}
\label{fig:latency_profile}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:latency_profile}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The mean online routing overhead was $\Delta t_{\text{route}} = 23.43\text{ ms}$, representing less than \textbf{0.33\%} of total end-to-end serving latency.
    \item Overall end-to-end latency dropped by \textbf{20.73\%} ($7.057\text{ s}$ vs. $8.902\text{ s}$), while Time-to-First-Token accelerated by \textbf{15.26\%} ($4.431\text{ s}$ vs. $5.229\text{ s}$) due to the faster execution speeds of Tier~1 and Tier~2 models.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Microarchitectural Scaling Analysis (Cross-Platform Framework)}
\label{subsec:scaling_analysis}

To quantify architectural energy efficiency across hardware generations, we define the Microarchitectural Energy Scaling Ratio $\eta$:
\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{E_{\text{net}}(\text{Platform B: RTX 3060 Ti})}{E_{\text{net}}(\text{Platform A: RTX 4060})}
\end{equation}
Platform A represents the TSMC 4N process node (Ada Lovelace, 115~W TDP), whereas Platform B represents the Samsung 8nm node (Ampere, 200~W TDP). Based on empirical baseline profiling on Platform A ($E_{\text{net}} = 1,922.88\text{ J}$) and nominal silicon power-density scaling models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dynamic power under load scales with operating voltage $V^2$ and clock frequency $f$.
    \item Ada Lovelace's TSMC 4N node provides approximately $1.6\times\text{--}1.8\times$ performance-per-watt advantage over Samsung 8nm Ampere silicon under equivalent FP16/INT4 tensor workload saturations.
    \item The projected scaling ratio $\eta_{\text{projected}} \approx 1.72$ underscores that while hardware generational node shrinks deliver baseline efficiency, dynamic architectural query routing provides an orthogonal, compounding \textbf{30.49\% energy reduction} within any given hardware platform generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary of Empirical Findings}
\label{subsec:findings_summary}

The empirical findings from our hardware benchmark confirm three primary research conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Energy Disparity Across Tiers}: A 1B parameter model executes low-complexity tasks with up to \textbf{17x lower Joules/token} than an 8B baseline, proving that uniform frontier model serving incurs massive needless energy penalties.
    \item \textbf{Low-Overhead Routing Viability}: Host-CPU feature extraction and classification execute in under $25\text{ ms}$, ensuring zero GPU memory bandwidth or compute pipeline interference.
    \item \textbf{Pareto-Optimal Operating Point}: The Adaptive Complexity Router establishes a superior Pareto efficiency frontier, reducing net dynamic electrical energy by \textbf{30.49\%} and total workload energy by \textbf{23.88\%} while preserving \textbf{90.31\%} of baseline model quality.
\end{enumerate}
